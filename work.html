Most organisations hold years of records collected for one purpose. Joined to the right public data, the same records can answer new questions: what's driving the results, how far to trust them, and where to act next.

Case study · conservation nonprofit

Three decades of volunteer stream surveys, put to work

The Izaak Walton League of America's Save Our Streams volunteers have sampled stream insects, salt and nitrate across the country since the 1990s. The records were public but hard to use: six survey types, forms that changed over the years, and home test sites mixed in with field sites. We cleaned and protected the data, joined it to eight public datasets, and built an interactive explorer the League can use.

How we enriched one dataset with eight others

Volunteer surveys say what a stream looks like on one day. They can't say why. We matched every survey to the watershed it drains, the date it was taken and the nearest professional sites, then pulled in what public agencies already know about that place and time.

Privacy came first

Many salt and nitrate kits are tested at volunteers' homes. Before any analysis, home sites got a generic label and their locations were rounded to about a kilometre, and personal details were never copied. Data problems were reported to the League rather than quietly fixed: blank tests recorded as zero, the same state written two ways, and one salt reading far beyond anything physically possible.
